Routine Guide

The 5-step morning routine I would start with after 40

Five steps in the order that lets each one do its job, plus what to leave out of the morning and save for night.

By Katherine
Updated October 2026
6 min read

A morning routine has a narrower job than most product shelves suggest. It needs to clean your skin without stripping it, give it some antioxidant help for the day, keep your barrier comfortable, and protect it from the sun. These are the five steps I would start with if my skin had just changed and I wanted a routine that holds up, in the order that lets each one work.

01
Step One

Cleanser: start clean, not stripped

In the morning you are mostly rinsing off what built up overnight: a little oil, sweat, and whatever you put on at bedtime. A gentle, fragrance-free cleanser handles that without pulling moisture out of your skin. If your face feels tight after you wash, the cleanser is too strong for morning use.

If your skin runs dry, or you used a rich night cream, a splash of lukewarm water is often enough on its own. Hot water and scrubby tools tend to leave midlife skin redder and drier, so I keep both out of my morning.

What to look for

Skin sits at a slightly acidic pH, roughly 4.5 to 5.5, and harsh, high-pH cleansers can loosen the lipids that hold moisture in. Words like hydrating, gentle, or cream cleanser on the label are a good sign, along with ingredients such as ceramides and glycerin.

02
Step Two

Hydrating toner: optional, and gentle

A toner is the most skippable step in a morning routine, and that is fine. The useful kind is a watery, hydrating formula with ingredients like glycerin, hyaluronic acid, or panthenol, pressed into damp skin right after cleansing. It can make dry skin feel more comfortable and helps the next layers spread evenly.

I leave exfoliating acids such as glycolic, lactic, and salicylic acid for evening. They make skin more sensitive to the sun, and they work just as well at night when you are not heading outside. My nighttime routine covers how often to use them.

03
Step Three

Vitamin C: your daytime antioxidant

Vitamin C is the one active I always put on in the morning. It helps neutralize some of the free radicals that sun and pollution create during the day, it supports collagen, and over time it can soften the look of dark spots. It works alongside sunscreen, never instead of it.

The most studied form, L-ascorbic acid, is also the fussiest. It breaks down with light and air, and a serum that has turned dark orange or brown has lost much of its strength. Look for 10 to 20% in an opaque or airless bottle. If L-ascorbic acid stings, gentler forms like ascorbyl glucoside or THD ascorbate are easier to live with, even though the evidence behind them is thinner. There is more in my vitamin C guide.

What the research shows

In a 2005 study in the Journal of Investigative Dermatology, adding ferulic acid to a serum with 15% vitamin C and 1% vitamin E made the formula more stable and roughly doubled the protection it gave skin against simulated sunlight, from about fourfold to about eightfold. That result is why so many vitamin C serums now pair the three together.

04
Step Four

Moisturizer: a light layer under sunscreen

A morning moisturizer keeps your barrier comfortable through the day and gives sunscreen a smooth surface to sit on. Ceramides, glycerin, and hyaluronic acid are all good signs on the label. Lighter lotions tend to work better under sunscreen than rich creams, which can pill or slide.

If your sunscreen is moisturizing enough on its own, you can skip this step. Plenty of women over 40 do well with a single SPF moisturizer in the morning, as long as they apply enough of it to get the protection on the label.

Why ceramides

Ceramides make up roughly half of the lipids between the cells in the outer layer of skin. Estrogen helps keep that layer hydrated, so as levels fall in perimenopause, many of us notice our skin losing water faster. A ceramide moisturizer helps close that gap.

05
Step Five

Sunscreen: always last, every day

If I could keep only one step, it would be this one. Sun exposure drives most of the visible changes we think of as skin aging, including fine lines, dark spots, and lost firmness, and it keeps undoing the work your evening products do overnight.

Apply it last, after moisturizer, and use more than feels natural: about a quarter teaspoon for your face, or two finger lengths if you include your neck. Choose a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, and reapply every two hours when you are outside. The new US filter bemotrizinol is starting to show up in lighter formulas, which I wrote about in my bemotrizinol guide, and my sunscreen guide compares mineral and chemical options.

What the research shows

In a 2013 trial in the Annals of Internal Medicine, 903 adults under 55 were followed for four and a half years. The people who used sunscreen every day showed 24% less skin aging than the people who used it whenever they felt like it.

The bottom line

A good morning routine can be short. Cleanse gently, add vitamin C, moisturize if you need it, and finish with plenty of sunscreen, with a hydrating toner in the mix only if your skin likes it. Once these steps feel automatic, you can add extras like an eye cream or a peptide serum, but I would wait until the basics are a habit.

Your evening routine is where the slower, repair-focused work happens, including retinoids and exfoliation. If you want the whole plan in one place, my Start Here page walks through what to do first and what to add later.
