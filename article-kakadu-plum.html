Kakadu plum in skincare: what I'm learning (and what I want to try).

The world's highest-vitamin-C fruit is showing up in more midlife formulas. Here's what I've been learning about the chemistry, the sourcing question US beauty press is not asking, and the products I want to test on my own skin.

By Katherine Moran
Published September 3, 2026
Reading time 11 min

I've been curious about Kakadu plum for a couple of months now. The ingredient keeps showing up in the midlife-facing formulas I get sent, usually paired with peptides, usually framed as the natural answer to a vitamin C serum. Every brand-owned page repeats the same headline (highest vitamin C in the world), and no editorial voice I trust has published the honest chemistry read yet. So I decided to do the research myself and write my way through it.

What follows is where I've gotten to over the last few weeks. What I think holds up, what I think gets overstated in beauty marketing, and the specific products I want to test on my own skin over the next couple of months. Consider it the version of the ingredient spotlight I would want to read before spending my own money on any of them.

What Kakadu plum is.

Kakadu plum is the fruit of Terminalia ferdinandiana, a tree native to the tropical savannah woodlands of Northern Australia, specifically the Northern Territory and the Kimberley region of Western Australia. The fruit is small, yellow-green, and roughly the size of an olive. Aboriginal Australians have used it as food and medicine for tens of thousands of years, with different Aboriginal language groups using different names (gubinge is the Bardi word, murunga in some Yolngu communities, billygoat plum in Australian English).

The fruit grows wild across a large geographic area and is now also cultivated on a smaller commercial scale. Most of the supply that reaches the international skincare market is wild-harvested by Aboriginal communities and Aboriginal-owned enterprises. That fact turns out to be important, and I will come back to it.

The vitamin C claim, honestly.

The headline holds up. Kakadu plum has the highest recorded natural vitamin C content of any fruit on Earth. Independent testing puts the concentration at somewhere between 1,000 and 5,000 milligrams per 100 grams of fruit, depending on the harvest year, the geographic origin, and the analytical method used. Oranges come in around 53 milligrams per 100 grams. That is a 20 to 100 times multiplier, and the number holds up across multiple independent sources. The vitamin C is present primarily as L-ascorbic acid, which is the same active form you get in a proper vitamin C serum.

This is where I have to be careful though, because it's the point at which beauty marketing tends to overstate the case. The vitamin C content of the raw fruit is not the same thing as the vitamin C efficacy of a finished skincare product. Topical L-ascorbic acid needs a set of specific conditions to do its job on skin: a pH around 3.5, protection from oxidation (usually through pairing with vitamin E and ferulic acid), a concentration high enough to penetrate the outer layer of skin (the research puts this at somewhere around 10 to 20 percent for L-ascorbic acid in the finished formula), a stable vehicle, and packaging that keeps light and air out.

A Kakadu plum extract in a moisturizer is usually somewhere between 0.1 and 2 percent of the finished product. Even if that extract is very high in vitamin C by weight, the vitamin C concentration in the final formula that reaches your skin ends up being a small fraction of what a well-formulated L-ascorbic acid serum delivers. So the "highest vitamin C in the world" claim is technically true about the fruit while saying almost nothing useful about the finished product. Where I've come out on this: Kakadu plum is a legitimate botanical antioxidant with a genuine ingredient story, and it does not replace a well-formulated vitamin C serum. It complements one.

Where I've come out

Kakadu plum is a strong secondary ingredient. On its own, it is not a primary vitamin C solution. The best formulas I've looked at so far use it the way it should be used, as a botanical antioxidant complement often stacked with peptides for a brightening-and-firming combination. When it is sold as a replacement for L-ascorbic acid, that is marketing.

What the research shows.

Most of the peer-reviewed research on Kakadu plum for skin is in vitro or in animal models rather than in humans. The demonstrated activities include antioxidant capacity (measurable and unsurprising given the ingredient composition), tyrosinase inhibition (the enzyme involved in melanin production, which is the mechanism behind pigmentation-fading claims), and anti-inflammatory effect. The ellagic acid and gallic acid content contribute here alongside the vitamin C. Ellagic acid in particular has some interesting brightening data in vitro that predates the beauty industry's discovery of the ingredient.

There is also early lab work on elastase and collagenase inhibition, which is the theoretical anti-aging angle. Human clinical trials specifically for cosmetic applications are sparse, and where they exist they are usually short, small, and brand-funded. Compare this to L-ascorbic acid, which has decades of independent human data behind it. The honest framing is that Kakadu plum is a promising botanical antioxidant with plausible mechanisms and limited human clinical validation. This is not a takedown of the ingredient, because almost every novel botanical in skincare sits at exactly this evidence tier. Kakadu plum is a legitimate ingredient that has not yet reached the evidence level of the established chemistry standards.

The sourcing question US beauty press is not asking.

This is the part of the story I found most interesting, and the reason I think Kakadu plum belongs in the Ingredient Truth series rather than sitting in a product roundup. Kakadu plum is a native Australian ingredient tied deeply to Aboriginal traditional knowledge, and the international beauty market has now built a supply chain on top of that traditional knowledge that varies wildly in how ethically it operates.

The Nagoya Protocol is an international framework governing access to biological resources and the sharing of benefits from their commercialization, especially when Indigenous traditional knowledge is involved. In Australia, the Northern Australia Aboriginal Kakadu Plum Alliance (NAAKPA) operates an Access and Benefit Sharing framework that puts Aboriginal enterprises at the center of the supply chain: they harvest, process, and directly sell to international buyers. Some beauty brands source through NAAKPA or through other Aboriginal-owned cooperatives. Others source through intermediaries that do not have clear Indigenous benefit-sharing structures. Others do not disclose their sourcing at all.

There is also a documented fraud problem. A 2025 ScienceDirect paper on provenance authentication flagged that adulterated or fraudulent Kakadu plum extracts are already circulating in international markets, because global demand has outpaced verified Australian supply. That means the "Kakadu plum extract" on your ingredient label may or may not be Kakadu plum at all, and it may or may not be sourced in a way that returns value to the Aboriginal communities whose traditional knowledge made the ingredient commercially valuable in the first place.

I do not think this means we should avoid the ingredient. I do think it means the brand sourcing is part of the ingredient story, and beauty press has mostly skipped over it. If I am going to spend money on a Kakadu plum product, I want to know that the fruit came from a verified Australian source through a supply chain that pays Aboriginal harvesters fairly. If a brand cannot answer that question when asked, take that as information.

What I want to try.

Here is where I've come out on my next step. I want to test two Kakadu plum products on my own skin over the next 60 days, one from the accessible tier and one from the mid-tier, so I can report back on whether the ingredient does anything for my midlife skin that my existing routine does not.

The INKEY List Kakadu Plum Vitamin C Brightening Serum as the accessible-tier test. This is the version most readers can afford to try, the sodium ascorbyl phosphate pairing is a reasonable formulation choice, and INKEY's ingredient disclosure is stronger than most. Morning use, before SPF, four to five times per week.

An Australian-brand mid-tier product that I'm still deciding between KORA Organics Noni Bright and Ere Perez Kakadu C Vitamin Elixir. My criteria are verified Australian sourcing, a disclosed extract concentration, and a formulation that isn't trying to pass the plum extract off as a full L-ascorbic acid replacement.

I'll keep everything else in my routine constant for the test window so I can attribute any changes to the plum products with reasonable confidence. Weekly photos in consistent light, honest documentation as I go, and a proper write-up at the end. If it does nothing, I'll say so. If it produces visible change on tone or brightness that my existing routine hasn't, I'll say that too. This is the Field Notes approach I use for every ingredient I haven't been running long enough to draw conclusions on.

What I still do not know.

There is plenty I want to be up front about not knowing yet. I do not know whether Kakadu plum will outperform a standard L-ascorbic acid serum for my specific skin over a 60-day window (my working hypothesis is that it will not, but I want the personal data before I write with any confidence on this). I do not know how the different formulation approaches (booster vs. standalone serum vs. multi-active compound) compare for penetration and result. I do not know how sensitive-skin-prone women respond to the ingredient across formulas. I do not know how Kakadu plum performs stacked with retinaldehyde or peptides in the same routine, though the combination is theoretically strong.

What I do know is that this is an ingredient worth understanding, that the sourcing question is one worth asking, and that the current US beauty press coverage of it is thinner than it should be. If you have been curious about Kakadu plum, that curiosity is warranted. Just do not let the "100 times the vitamin C of oranges" headline convince you that a plum extract in a moisturizer is a substitute for a properly formulated vitamin C serum. It is a promising addition to a stack, not a replacement for the chemistry you already know works.

Questions I've been getting.

What is Kakadu plum in skincare?

Kakadu plum is the fruit of Terminalia ferdinandiana, a small yellow-green fruit native to Northern Australia. It has the highest recorded vitamin C content of any fruit, roughly 20 to 100 times that of oranges, present primarily as L-ascorbic acid. In skincare it is used as a botanical antioxidant and brightening ingredient, often paired with peptides.

Does Kakadu plum work as well as a vitamin C serum?

Not necessarily. The vitamin C content of the raw fruit is impressive, but topical efficacy depends on the finished formulation, not the source ingredient. A plum extract at low percentages in a moisturizer does not clinically match a well-formulated 10 to 20 percent L-ascorbic acid serum at pH 3.5. Use it as a complement, not a substitute.

Is Kakadu plum ethically sourced?

It depends on the brand. The Northern Australia Aboriginal Kakadu Plum Alliance (NAAKPA) operates an Access and Benefit Sharing framework consistent with the Nagoya Protocol, ensuring Aboriginal harvesters are at the center of the supply chain. Some brands source through NAAKPA and similar Indigenous cooperatives, others do not disclose their sourcing, and there is documented fraud in the international market.

Which brands use Kakadu plum in skincare?

Across price tiers: The INKEY List at the accessible end, INIKA Organic and Ere Perez in the mid-tier, KORA Organics and Go-To Skincare from the Australian brand cluster, and U Beauty at the luxury tier. A growing indie tier pairs it with peptides for a brightening-plus-firming stack.

Can I use Kakadu plum with retinol?

Yes, in the standard pattern of vitamin C in the morning and retinol at night. If you introduce a Kakadu plum product to your routine, do it alone for two weeks before adding anything else, especially if your skin runs reactive. That way you can attribute any response to the correct ingredient.

The bottom line

Promising, worth trying, not a vitamin C serum replacement.

Kakadu plum is a legitimate botanical antioxidant with a specific chemistry story, strong combination potential when it is paired with peptides, and a sourcing question that beauty press has mostly ignored. Worth trying if you are curious. Not a substitute for a properly formulated vitamin C serum, and any brand telling you otherwise is selling you the headline rather than the ingredient. I am running my own 60-day test now and will report back. Ask about the sourcing before you buy.
