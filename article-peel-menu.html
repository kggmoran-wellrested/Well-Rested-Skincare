The peel menu, from daily-gentle to once-a-year.

I am about to try an at-home TCA 13% peel and document the whole thing. Before I did, I wanted to understand everything I was choosing between. This is the full peel menu, from the gentlest daily acid to the deepest in-office treatment, and where I landed.

By Katherine Moran
Published October 2026
12 min read

I am going to try an at-home TCA 13% peel in the next few weeks, and I want to document the whole thing. The before, the during, the after. If it goes well, you will know. If it does not, you will know that too.

Before I committed to that, I wanted to understand everything I was choosing between. What a peel does, how a TCA peel is different from the glycolic toner I have been using for years, and whether a home version of a procedure usually done at the dermatologist's office is a good idea or a bad one. This article is the research I did before making that call.

Here is the simple part. A chemical peel is an acid, put on the skin, that dissolves the bonds holding dead cells on the surface. The dead layer sheds and the newer skin underneath shows up. That is the whole idea.

What makes it feel complicated is that "peel" covers a huge range. On the gentle end, there is a daily lactic acid you can use before bed and barely feel. On the strong end, there is a phenol peel done in a hospital under anesthesia that resurfaces skin so deeply you look like a different person when it heals. Everything in between does something a little different.

I am going to walk through them from gentlest to strongest, so if you are also deciding what level makes sense for your skin, you have the full menu. Then at the end I will share where I landed on the TCA 13% specifically and what I am doing to prep. If you know more about any of these than I do, tell me. I am figuring this out alongside you, not telling you what to do.

A peel is not one thing.

Before the menu, the vocabulary. There are three major categories of acid used in peels, and they do different jobs because they move through skin differently.

AHAs are alpha hydroxy acids. They are water-soluble, which means they work on the skin's surface. The main AHAs are glycolic, lactic, mandelic, and tartaric. They differ mostly by molecule size, which determines how deeply they penetrate at the same concentration.

BHA is beta hydroxy acid, almost always salicylic acid. It is oil-soluble, which lets it penetrate into the pore itself rather than sitting on top of skin. This is why salicylic acid is the acid you want for breakouts and blackheads.

PHAs are polyhydroxy acids, the newest category. The main PHAs are gluconolactone and lactobionic acid. They work on the surface like AHAs but have much larger molecules, so they penetrate more slowly. This makes them the gentlest option available and the one that reactive, perimenopausal, and barrier-compromised skin can usually tolerate when nothing else works.

The quick reference
  • AHA = surface exfoliation, water-soluble (glycolic, lactic, mandelic)
  • BHA = pore exfoliation, oil-soluble (salicylic)
  • PHA = gentle surface exfoliation, large molecule (gluconolactone)
  • TCA = medium-depth, in-office acid (trichloroacetic)
  • Phenol = deep peel, surgical setting (carbolic acid)

Do peels work?

The short answer is yes, with one caveat. Peels are one of the most well-studied categories in dermatology, and the clinical data is strong across the board. The caveat is that the result scales with the depth of the peel, and so does the recovery.

A daily PHA will not transform your skin. It will protect it, support cell turnover, and prevent the kind of surface buildup that makes skin look dull. The benefit is maintenance.

A weekly glycolic or multi-acid blend will produce visible brightening and smoother texture within three to four weeks of consistent use. The benefit is active improvement.

An in-office Jessner's or TCA peel will produce a change you can see in the mirror and in photos within two weeks. The benefit is a leap forward, which skincare alone cannot match.

A phenol peel resurfaces skin so deeply that the result rivals surgical procedures for photoaging. The benefit is a once-in-a-lifetime reset, at the cost of a significant recovery and permanent changes to skin tone.

The question is not whether peels work. The question is which level of result justifies the level of commitment for your skin, your schedule, and your risk tolerance.

The menu, gentlest to strongest.

Now the peels themselves, in order of how aggressively they act on the skin.

01 · Gentlest

PHA (Gluconolactone, Lactobionic Acid)

The daily acid for skin that cannot tolerate anything else.

PHAs are the largest acid molecules used in skincare. They sit on the surface and work slowly, which makes them safe for daily use on reactive skin, rosacea-adjacent skin, perimenopausal skin, and anyone recovering from barrier damage. They also provide mild antioxidant and humectant activity, which no other acid does.

Results are the slowest of any peel category, but the trade-off is tolerability. You can layer PHAs over almost anything without irritation.

Best forSensitive, reactive, or perimenopausal skin
FrequencyDaily, AM or PM
At homeYes
DowntimeNone
02

Mandelic Acid

The gentle AHA for sensitive skin and darker skin tones.

Mandelic is an AHA, but the molecule is larger than glycolic or lactic, which slows its penetration and reduces irritation. It is often recommended for melasma-prone or deeper skin tones because it has a lower risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation than more aggressive acids.

Also has mild antibacterial properties, so it does double duty for hormonal breakouts in perimenopause.

Best forMelasma, sensitive skin, darker skin tones
Frequency2 to 3 nights per week
At homeYes
DowntimeNone
03

Lactic Acid

The AHA that moisturizes while it exfoliates.

Lactic acid is an AHA that is also a humectant, which means it hydrates skin at the same time it dissolves dead cells. This is the acid I recommend most often for anyone starting chemical exfoliation for the first time in their 40s. Perimenopausal skin that has become dry responds better to lactic than to glycolic.

At 5 to 10 percent, it is a gentle daily or every-other-day acid. At 10 to 20 percent, it is a weekly peel-style treatment.

Best forDry, dull, or first-time peel skin
Frequency2 to 4 nights per week
At homeYes
DowntimeNone at low concentrations
04

Salicylic Acid (BHA)

The oil-soluble acid for pores and breakouts.

Salicylic acid is the only common BHA. Because it is oil-soluble, it penetrates into the pore itself rather than working on the surface. This is why it is the acid for breakouts, blackheads, and clogged pores, including the hormonal breakouts that show up along the jawline in perimenopause.

Also has anti-inflammatory properties, which makes it useful for rosacea-adjacent redness and for calming active acne lesions.

Best forBreakouts, blackheads, clogged pores
Frequency2 to 3 nights per week
At homeYes, up to 2 percent
DowntimeNone
05

Glycolic Acid

The strongest AHA you can safely use at home.

Glycolic has the smallest molecule of any AHA, which means it penetrates deeper than lactic or mandelic at the same concentration. It also carries the highest irritation risk at the same concentration. The trade-off is faster, more visible results on texture, tone, and mild pigmentation.

At home, 7 to 10 percent is the range I recommend for perimenopausal skin two to three nights a week. In-office, glycolic peels run 20 to 70 percent and require provider supervision. For a budget-friendly introduction, The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% Toning Solution is one of the best-value chemical exfoliants on the market.

Best forDullness, texture, mild hyperpigmentation
Frequency2 to 3 nights per week
At homeYes, up to 15 percent
DowntimeNone at home strengths
06

Multi-Acid Blends

The at-home peel step with built-in buffering.

Multi-acid formulas combine two or three acids so the strongest one is buffered by the gentler ones. This gives you most of the benefit of a stronger single-acid product with less irritation. The format tends to be creamy or lotion-based rather than toner-based, which also slows the acid absorption.

The Prequel Multi Acid Milk Peel (glycolic plus lactic in a milky base) is the one I use most often. The Obagi CLENZIderm Resurfacing Multi-Acid Exfoliating Peel Pads are a stronger option for anyone whose skin is already tolerant to acids. Both are weekly rather than daily products.

Best forExperienced users, weekly reset
Frequency1 to 2 times per week
At homeYes
DowntimeNone to one day of mild flaking
07 · In-office

Jessner's Solution

The classic in-office peel for pigmentation and texture.

Jessner's is a traditional blend of salicylic acid, lactic acid, and resorcinol applied in multiple layers in-office. Each layer increases the depth of the peel. Your provider stops when the skin shows the right level of frosting or whitening on the surface.

Results are faster and more dramatic than any at-home product. Expect three to seven days of peeling, mild redness for a week, and visible improvement in tone and texture at the two-week mark.

Best forPigmentation, texture, acne scarring
FrequencyEvery 4 to 6 weeks, 3 to 6 session series
At homeNo
Downtime3 to 7 days of peeling
08

TCA Peel (10 to 20 percent)

The medium-depth in-office peel for serious resurfacing.

Trichloroacetic acid is one of the strongest peels used routinely in dermatology. At 10 to 20 percent, it treats fine lines, moderate pigmentation, and some early photoaging. Results are visible, and the recovery is significant.

Expect five to seven days of visible peeling and redness, and two to three weeks before skin looks fully recovered. Strict SPF and barrier support throughout. This is a fall-and-winter peel because UV exposure during healing is the biggest risk factor for post-peel hyperpigmentation.

Best forFine lines, moderate pigmentation, early photoaging
FrequencyAnnually or every 6 to 12 months
At homeNo
Downtime7 to 14 days visible
09

TCA Cross and Deeper TCA (25 to 35 percent)

The targeted or deeper medium-depth treatment.

TCA at 25 to 35 percent is used two ways. As a full-face medium-depth peel for deeper photoaging and texture. Or as TCA CROSS, where a provider applies small drops of high-concentration TCA directly into icepick acne scars to prompt collagen remodeling at the base of each one.

Recovery for a full-face deeper TCA runs 10 to 14 days visible and three to four weeks of sensitive, pink skin. For TCA CROSS, recovery is site-specific and much shorter. Both are once-a-year or less-often treatments.

Best forDeeper photoaging, acne scarring, targeted work
FrequencyOnce a year at most
At homeNo
Downtime10 to 14 days visible
10 · Most extreme

Phenol (Baker-Gordon Formula)

The deep peel done once in a lifetime, in a hospital.

Phenol peels are the deepest chemical peel available. They penetrate into the mid-dermis and produce dramatic resurfacing that softens deep wrinkles, reverses significant photoaging, and replaces the top layers of skin with new tissue. Performed under anesthesia in a hospital or surgical setting, often by a plastic surgeon.

Recovery runs two to three weeks of significant visible healing and three to six months of continued redness that gradually fades. Permanent lightening of treated areas is common, which is why this peel is almost never done on anyone with medium to deeper skin tones. Most patients do this once in a lifetime, if at all.

Best forDeep wrinkles, significant photoaging
FrequencyOnce in a lifetime
At homeNo
Downtime2 to 3 weeks visible, months of healing

How to pick the right peel for your skin.

With ten options on the menu, the question becomes which one is right for you. The honest framework is simple.

If you have never used a chemical exfoliant, start with lactic acid at a low concentration two nights a week. Give your skin four weeks to adjust before adding anything stronger. The lactic acid also hydrates, which cushions the learning curve.

If your main concern is dullness and texture, glycolic acid at 7 to 10 percent, two to three nights a week, is the fastest-moving at-home option.

If your main concern is breakouts, blackheads, or clogged pores, salicylic acid is the acid. BHA formulas at 1 to 2 percent used two to three nights a week.

If your main concern is pigmentation or melasma, start with mandelic, which has the lowest risk of worsening pigmentation. In-office Jessner's peels are also a strong option under provider supervision. The pigmentation stack guide covers the full brightening protocol that pairs with gentle peels.

If your skin is sensitive, reactive, or barrier-compromised, start with PHAs. They are the gentlest acid available and will not push a stressed barrier further off balance.

If you want serious change beyond what at-home peels can produce, the conversation is in-office. Start with Jessner's or 10 percent TCA for a one-session trial. Save deeper TCA or phenol for consultation with a board-certified dermatologist or plastic surgeon.

The right peel is the strongest one your skin can tolerate consistently. Consistency moves the needle. Occasional heroic use does not.

The perimenopause adjustment.

If you are reading this and you are over 40, there is one adjustment to make to anything you did in your 30s.

Perimenopausal skin has a thinner barrier, slower cell turnover recovery, and higher pigmentation reactivity. The glycolic toner you used three nights a week at 35 can leave you with a persistent dark patch at 48. The at-home peel pads that gave you a glow in your mid-30s can trigger weeks of flaking in your late 40s.

Three practical adjustments. First, drop the concentration you used ten years ago and go up only if your skin tolerates it well. Second, add a hydrating lactic acid to your rotation even if you prefer stronger acids, because the barrier support counts for more now. Third, if you are planning any in-office peel, schedule it in October through March when UV exposure is low. See the fall skincare reset guide for the full seasonal framework.

How to incorporate a peel into your routine.

A peel does not stand alone. It is a swap into an existing routine, not a replacement for it. Here is how it fits.

A sample weekly rotation for at-home peels. Monday: retinoid. Tuesday: peel (lactic, glycolic, or multi-acid). Wednesday: peptide or growth factor serum only. Thursday: retinoid. Friday: peel. Saturday and Sunday: barrier recovery with ceramides, hyaluronic acid, and a rich moisturizer. SPF 50 every morning without exception. This gives your skin three recovery nights, two active resurfacing nights, and two active turnover nights per week.

Do not stack acids with retinoids on the same night. Both resurface, and the combination overwhelms the barrier. Alternate retinoid nights and peel nights with at least one recovery night in between.

SPF the next morning is non-optional. Every acid on the list increases UV sensitivity for a week after use. Skip the SPF and you undo the result.

Buffer with moisture. Apply the acid first, let it absorb for 30 seconds, then follow with a hydrating serum and a ceramide moisturizer. Dryness and flaking are signs you are being too aggressive, not that the peel is working.

Avoid the eye area, the corners of the nose, and the corners of the mouth. Skin is thinnest in these zones and most reactive to any acid.

Pause before in-office procedures. Stop at-home peels one week before any in-office treatment and resume one week after your provider clears you. The same applies before a Jessner's or TCA peel if you decide to go in-office.

When to call your provider

If skin stays red for more than 24 hours after an at-home peel, if you see darkening of a patch where you applied the acid, if a chemical peel-related reaction looks worse at 48 hours than at 24, or if you develop a cold sore in response to a peel, pause everything and contact your dermatologist. The barrier is telling you to stop.

What I am about to try: a TCA 13% at home.

Here is where I landed, and what I am going to document.

I am going to do a TCA 13% peel at home over the next few weeks. 13% is at the lower end of what is considered a superficial TCA peel. Light enough that I feel comfortable doing it myself as a nurse who knows what the skin should look like at each stage, strong enough that I expect to see a visible change in tone and texture.

The reason I am going this route instead of booking something in-office is honestly curiosity. I want to see what a TCA at this strength does on my skin, in my hands, without the price point of a provider peel. I also like the idea of being able to repeat it at a lower frequency rather than booking a once-a-year treatment. If I have a complication, I will see my dermatologist. I am not pretending this is risk-free.

What I am doing to prep.

Pausing retinoids one full week before. Dropping any at-home glycolic, lactic, or multi-acid during that same week so my barrier is calm when I start. Front-loading barrier support with ceramides and a rich moisturizer. Taking a before photo in natural light at three angles.

What I am watching for.

Frosting on the surface, which is the sign the acid has done its work. Even application. Any patches that feel too hot or sting more than the rest, which would mean I need to neutralize that spot faster. The 48-hour mark is when I will know if I pushed it too hard. If the redness lingers past 72 hours, I will pause the whole plan and check in with my dermatologist.

What I expect at best.

Three to five days of light peeling. Brighter, smoother skin by day seven. Softer fine lines by week two. A visible shift in the tone of a few sun spots I have been working on for a year.

What I would not try at home.

Anything above TCA 15%. Any Jessner's solution, which requires provider timing. Anything near the eye area at any strength. Any TCA CROSS or spot treatment on scars, which I would absolutely do in an office with someone who has done it hundreds of times.

I will report back

I am going to film the prep, the peel day, and the recovery, and share everything in a follow-up article and on Instagram. If you have been curious about trying one yourself, you will see what the honest version looks like. If I run into trouble, that goes on the page too. The point of this is to figure out whether a home TCA is a reasonable step for midlife skin, or whether it should stay in the dermatologist's office. I do not know the answer yet.

Who should skip peels entirely.

Chemical peels are safe for most people, but not everyone.

Skip peels if you are pregnant or nursing, if you have active rosacea or eczema flaring, if you have a recent sunburn, or if you have used isotretinoin (Accutane) in the past six months. If you have a history of cold sores, talk to your provider about a prophylactic antiviral before any in-office peel.

Deeper skin tones have additional considerations for medium and deep peels. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is a higher risk, which is why many providers prefer Jessner's or mandelic for darker skin and skip phenol entirely. A board-certified dermatologist experienced with your skin type is the right person to design the plan.
